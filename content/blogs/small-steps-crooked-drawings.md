
The flashcard says 猫 and you've seen it eleven times this week. Still nothing. You flip it over, read *neko*, cat, and feel a small flash of heat in your face before sliding it to the back of the pile.

Somewhere else, someone is drawing their two hundredth bounding box around a dog. The model still thinks the dog is a couch. The tea beside their keyboard went cold an hour ago and has grown a skin.

And somewhere else again, someone is singing scales in the car with the windows up. The high note cracks every time. They sing it anyway, and then once more at the red light.

Three different rooms. The same quiet voice in each of them. *I'm not good at this yet.*

## Kaizen

The Japanese have a word for what all three of them are doing. Kaizen. 改善. *Kai*, change. *Zen*, good. Change for the better.

It became famous on factory floors, where any worker could stop the line if something looked off. Not to blame anyone. To fix one small thing, today, and another small thing tomorrow. Nobody was waiting for a miracle. They were just tightening bolts.

The language learner doesn't need to be fluent this month. They need 猫 today and 犬 tomorrow. Five words a day doesn't sound like much until you notice it's nearly two thousand by the end of the year.

The computer vision learner doesn't need a perfect model. They need one better augmentation, one cleaner label, one more look at the images the model got wrong. Accuracy goes from 61% to 62%. It's boring. That's the point.

The singer doesn't need to hit the top of the song tonight. Half a step higher than last week is enough. Then half a step more.

Kaizen asks a smaller question. Not *how do I get great?* but *what's one thing I can do a little better today?* The big question is too heavy to carry every morning. The small one fits in your pocket, and you can answer it every single day.

But there's a trap hiding in all those small steps.

Get good at kaizen and your eyes start doing something strange. They only see the gap. 62% is just 38% wrong. Two thousand words is nothing next to the ones you still don't know. The note you hit doesn't count, only the one that cracked.

## Mederu

There's another word. 愛でる. Mederu. It's built on 愛, love. To cherish. To look at something with affection, the way people sit under cherry blossoms knowing they'll be gone in a week. Not to fix it. Not to improve it. Just to enjoy it while it's here.

There's a drawing on a fridge somewhere right now. Crayon. The house has no door. The sun has a face and far too many teeth. The dog is bigger than the house.

Nobody would call it good. It's been up there for three years. Every morning someone reaches past it for the milk, smiles, and doesn't once think about how the perspective could be improved.

That's mederu. The drawing isn't loved because it's finished. It's loved because of who made it and when. One day that kid will draw a proper house with a proper door, and this toothy sun will never exist again.

We do this for children without thinking. We almost never do it for ourselves.

Your 62% model is a crayon drawing. So is your clumsy first sentence in a new language, and the cracked note in the car. They aren't failures waiting to be replaced. They're the drawings on the fridge of the person you're becoming.

## Both, not one

Kaizen looks at what could be. Mederu looks at what is.

One makes the next drawing better. The other lets you love this one. Kaizen without mederu is a hunger that never gets fed, because there's always another percent. Mederu without kaizen is a fridge that never gets a new drawing.

So learn the word today, then say it out loud and enjoy that you know it. Fix the labels, then look at everything the model already gets right. Sing the scale, then let yourself smile at the notes that landed.

Months from now you'll be better at all of it. The sentences will flow. The model will ship. The high note will just be a note.

Keep the old drawings anyway. Stick them on the fridge.

Toothy sun and all.
