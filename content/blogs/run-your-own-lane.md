Before a race, someone straps blinders onto the horse. Leather flaps, bolted to the bridle, angled just enough that the animal stops registering the crowd in the stands, the horse running beside it, the whole noisy circus pressing in from every side. All that's left in its field of vision is the lane directly ahead. I think about that image more than I probably should, because at twenty-six I own the opposite problem. I've got no blinders at all. Just a wide-open field of vision with everything in it shouting for a piece of my week.

System design. Low-level design, then the high-level kind. AWS, sitting there half-studied. Node.js. Plain JavaScript, which I keep telling myself I should get back to basics on. Backend work. Databases I've only poked at. A frontend stack I've never properly committed to. Three or four new project ideas a month, minimum. A startup concept that won't leave me alone. Workouts I keep rescheduling. A diet I keep renegotiating with myself. Startup events I RSVP to and then talk myself out of. Writing, when I remember I enjoy it. A book collection that has quietly become a small hoarding problem. And then, out of nowhere this year, an entire new field, AR and VR, that grabbed me by the collar for reasons I still can't fully explain.

There's a recipe I've been meaning to cook for months and somehow never do. A craft project sitting half-bought in a drawer. Video editing software I installed and never opened. A UI/UX file open in another tab right now, this exact minute, judging me. Algorithmic trading, which I understand in theory and have never once acted on. Futures and options, same story. And that's not even the full count: that's just what surfaces when I sit still long enough to let it.

That's a lot of horses yoked to one cart, all pulling in their own direction.

So here's the line I keep handing myself, on the days the cart threatens to come apart at the axle:

Run your own lane.

Right now I'm running it toward one specific thing, and it happens to be the most fun I've had in a while.

I'm building a fintech project. Peel off the pitch-deck language and what's left is: automate wealth management with a handful of AI agents instead of a human advisor. Goal planning. Retirement projections. Health insurance. Loan prepayments. Even planning for a kid's education, years before that kid is anything more than a maybe. The kind of conversation that usually takes several expensive sessions with a certified human, except here it's a cluster of agents talking to each other, quietly doing the arithmetic a person would normally charge you for.

It's partially working, which is its own kind of satisfying. The retirement agent gives projections that actually hold up against a napkin calculation. The goal-planning one reasons through trade-offs the way a decent advisor would. The insurance agent and the loan-prepayment agent, though, still trip over edge cases I haven't fully mapped yet: a policy exclusion here, a prepayment penalty there, the kind of mess that looks simple until you're the one coding it.

I could hand you a tidy list of reasons I'm building it. The market's enormous right now. The timing, with everyone suddenly fintech-curious, couldn't be better. It would look sharp on a resume, too, if I'm being honest about the vanity angle.

But none of that is the actual reason.

The actual reason is: because. Because I wanted to find out if it could be done at all, and the only way to find that out was to start building it before I was sure.

Back to the lane.

It's one of the lines I keep returning to this year, and probably every year after this one too, because the problem it answers doesn't go away once you solve it once.

Here's the trap, and it's an easy one to fall into: you can convince yourself that the responsible move is to research every step before taking the first one. Read every system design blog in existence before writing a single line of your own. Watch every AWS comparison video on the internet before you ever work up the nerve to register for the exam. There's always one more article, one more framework comparison, one more "things I wish I knew before starting" post standing between you and actually starting.

And now, on top of all that, you can type the whole plan into a box and have something far smarter than you hand it back fully formed: the way a high schooler gets an essay written for them that exactly two people will ever read, the mother and the teacher, both of whom already suspect it wasn't really written by the kid. We all know, somewhere underneath the convenience, that the value was supposed to live in the struggling-through, not in the clean finished page someone else generated for us.

What a strange time to be alive, genuinely. You type a question into a box and the accumulated knowledge of the species looks back at you, not as a static reference but as something that argues with you, interactive and immediate.

And with that much borrowed knowledge comes a strange, inverted kind of power. More information should make a person more capable. Sometimes it does the opposite.

Too much knowledge, like most things, is dosage dependent. Past a certain point it stops being fuel and quietly turns into a brake.

A head stuffed with too much knowledge becomes excellent at listing every reason a thing can't be done. A head with a little less of it just asks: what if it could?

Waiting around for one more fact can genuinely hold you in place for months. You tell yourself, if I just research a little longer, the answer will present itself and I'll finally know how to begin.

Sometimes that's even true.

But more often, the real reason the answer hasn't shown up is that you don't have the right question yet, not really. And the right question refuses to show itself until you're already a few steps into the walk. It hides from anyone still standing at the trailhead reading the map.

So you try something. You tinker a little here, mix a little there, half-think through a problem while brushing your teeth at eleven at night. You finally cook the recipe you'd been circling for months, and it doesn't come out the way the photo promised: turns out there wasn't nearly enough salt for your taste, and the recipe never mentioned you're supposed to add a second round ten minutes in so it doesn't just dissolve into the sauce. Your dinner guests end up quietly salting their own plates on top of what you already put in, nobody says a word about it, and collectively the table eats a week's sodium in one sitting.

Let's bring this back around.

The lesson, such as it is: run your own lane.

But know going in that this doesn't guarantee you'll know what to do at every turn of it. You'll talk yourself into knots far more often than necessary unless you make peace, early, with not having the whole map laid out before you move.

There's a line from Richard Feynman I keep returning to. Nobody has ever actually worked out what life is about, he said, and that's fine. It doesn't need to be worked out. Find something that genuinely interests you and keep doing it, because nearly anything turns out to be fascinating once you go deep enough into it.

What I like is that he didn't say find the right thing. He said find an interesting thing. The rightness, apparently, sorts itself out somewhere down the line, or it simply doesn't matter as much as everyone insists it does.

There's a second thing attributed to Feynman, in one phrasing or another: if you can't explain what you're doing to someone who's never heard of it, you probably understand it less than you think you do. I believe that one just as much. It's easy enough to deploy the right vocabulary around a subject. It's much harder to make a stranger, with zero background, nod and say, oh, okay, that actually makes sense.

So move a little. Sway with whatever improvised, unscored rhythm the world happens to be playing that day.

Because how dull would any of this be if you already knew every outcome in advance?

Do the thing. Take the shot. Trust that you'll figure the rest out while it's happening, not before.

Those end up being the best stories to hear secondhand: the ones where the person clearly had no idea how it would turn out and kept going regardless.

They're even better to be the one living them.

So, again: run your own lane.

Go cook the recipe you keep rescheduling. Salt it properly this time, and salt it twice.

Learn system design the way anyone actually learns anything: badly, slowly, by starting the low-level design before you feel remotely ready for the high-level one.

Open the AWS console and build the small, ugly, barely-functional thing instead of watching one more comparison video about it.

Sketch the AR/VR idea even though you've never touched the field before today.

Start the craft project. Open the video editor. Open the UI/UX file that's been sitting in that tab, quietly judging you, for three weeks now.

Place the trade in the algorithmic strategy instead of backtesting it for the hundredth time this month.

Chase the startup idea you keep talking yourself out of over coffee, and trust that you're sharper in practice than you sound on paper.

Go to the startup event you keep RSVPing to and bailing on, and actually talk to one stranger while you're there.

Write the thing sitting in your drafts. Read the book that's been waiting in that collection since you bought it with good intentions.

The world gets noticeably more interesting the moment you do things instead of endlessly planning to.

If you're stuck, let motion be the cure, not more thinking.

Make something. Write something. Code something. Cook something, properly salted this time. Walk somewhere you haven't walked before. Lift something heavier than you lifted last week. Call the person you've been meaning to call since last month.

For the horse pulling against its own blinders.

For the twenty-six-year-old with a cart full of horses and nowhere near enough hands.

For you, reading this instead of doing the thing you already know you should be doing.

Run your own lane.
