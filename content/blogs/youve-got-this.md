
A friend sends you something they made. A song recorded on their phone, the first chapter of a book, a website that barely loads, a business idea typed out at midnight. And now there's a choice sitting right there in your hands.

You can cheer them on. Or you can tell them everything that's wrong with it. Both take about the same number of words, but only one of them takes any real effort.

Cheering someone on asks more of you than it looks. You put their thing ahead of your own for a while. You give your time, your attention, sometimes your name, because if it flops you were the one who said go for it. That's a real risk. Not just in your head, but in your evenings and your energy too.

And it's a skill. Good support has to be practised. You learn how to give feedback that helps instead of hurts, when to push and when to back off. And you stick around for when it doesn't work.

Getting good at it takes a lot of getting it wrong. Saying too much. Saying too little. It takes trust both ways, they have to believe you mean it and you have to believe they'll keep going. It takes a leap, putting yourself behind something that isn't finished yet. And it takes paying attention. Noticing the small drop in their voice when they talk about it. Noticing when the excitement starts to fade before they've even said a word.

---

Criticism doesn't need any practice. Anyone can do it from day one. It's easy to point at what's broken.

Think of someone sharing their thing for the very first time. Nervous. Not sure if it's any good. One offhand comment is all it takes. Wouldn't work. Who'd pay for that? Seen it before. And the thing they'd been working on quietly for weeks stops right there.

Being a supporter isn't just liking the post. Anyone can type "nice!" and keep scrolling. The real kind gets involved. They test the app. They read the second draft. They ask what's next and they actually mean it. They get better at helping and they put the other person first. And afterwards they think about how they could've helped better.

The critic rarely says anything about where the thing could go. They judge it exactly as it is today and walk off. No next step, no what if, just a verdict.

But anyone who's made anything knows this. The current version of a creation is often the worst it's ever going to be. First drafts are supposed to be rough.

Or maybe it's finished. Maybe they made exactly what they wanted to make and stopped. A big part of making things is knowing when they're done, and that's their call, not yours.

---

In so many moments in life, you get the chance to be the one who cheers or the one who criticises. It happens at work, at dinner, in the group chat. Most days you'll get the chance to be both.

Do I want to cheer this person on? Put them ahead of me, practise, get better together. Am I ready to be there if it doesn't work out?

Or do I want to pick it apart? Find the holes, ask why they'd even bother doing it in the first place (people who make things know there doesn't need to be a reason). Point out all the ways it won't work, like they haven't already lain awake thinking about every single one of them.

One takes effort. Consideration. A dedication to practising and improving and getting stronger. The audacity to say, you've got this, let's do it. And then following up next week to see how it's going.

The other gets to feel smart in the moment. Applauding themselves for pointing out the obvious. The thing they found was never hidden.

One gets to improve and have fun together. Both people come out of it better than they went in. The other has fun at the expense of someone else.

---

I know which one I'd rather be.

Tell me your dreams and I'll say it. And I'll say it again. And again after that.

You've got this.

Ha!

Oh yes, you've got this!

Go and make magic!

---

And yes, there's a time to be a critic. When someone is doing something that's plain wrong, someone should say so. Those things are easy to critique.

I'm not talking about those. I'm talking about the fragile part. The creating, the making, the taking a risk, the tiptoeing into the unknown with no idea how it'll turn out.

So the next time that message comes through, the song, the chapter, the website that barely loads, I already know what I'll write back.

To that, I clap you on.
